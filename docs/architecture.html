§00 · Thesis

One idea carries the whole system

Every workflow step is the same shape: { capability, action, provider }. A founder declares what they want done; the platform decides who does it. That single abstraction is simultaneously your integration strategy, your "third-party now, first-party later" path, and your product line. The workflow never names a vendor — it depends on a port, and a registry resolves the port to whichever adapter the founder chose.

This makes the codebase split cleanly along product-line-engineering lines: a large, reusable platform and a thin band of per-workflow and per-provider variation.

~70%  Core platform — engine · company object · services · ports · infra
~30%  Variant — workflow manifests · adapters · rule-packs

Three load-bearing decisions sit under everything: the orchestrator uses durable execution (workflows wait days and survive restarts), the Company Object is event-sourced (documents stay in sync, the audit trail is free), and the system ships as one deployable with hard internal seams.

§02 · Source of truth

The Company Object, event-sourced

Your marketing promises documents that "regenerate and stay in sync." That is not a mutable record you overwrite — it is an append-only event log with projections. Every change ("address updated," "founder added," "EIN issued") is an immutable event. Projections re-derive the cap table, the documents, the compliance calendar, and the cross-workflow triggers from those events. Change the registered address once, and every generated document that embeds it is regenerated from the same source.

§03 · The AI layer

Document intelligence is the product

Half your workflow nodes say "generate" — Articles of Incorporation, the 83(b) election, offer letters, positioning statements. A hallucinated clause in a legal or financial document is not a bug, it is a liability. So generation is never free-form. It runs a hard pipeline: ground the model in a vetted template and clause library plus the Company Object's fields, generate through a model port, validate deterministically (schema, required fields, jurisdiction rules), gate on a human approval (your existing "Approve?" steps), then write to an immutable, versioned store. An eval suite of golden documents guards every change.

The model is just another port — the same abstraction as banking or e-sign — so you can route by document type, fall back across providers, and track cost per tenant without touching workflow code.

§04 · Compliance

Regulatory intelligence, as a first-class engine

Compliance is a pillar in your deck, so it gets real architecture, not a cron job. The engine is driven by jurisdiction rule-packs — data, not code — that describe what's required for a given entity type and state. A regulatory-data pipeline keeps those packs current as laws change. From a tenant's profile it generates the deadline calendar and the recurring filings, and it owns the irreversible fuses.

The 83(b) fuse. A 30-day, no-extensions, irreversible deadline cannot ride on best-effort delivery. It needs redundant, multi-channel, acknowledged reminders with escalating urgency and a human backstop if it goes unacknowledged. Treat it as the canonical guaranteed-delivery case the whole notification path is designed around.

  • Rule-packs are versioned variation assets — adding a jurisdiction is authoring data, not shipping code.
  • Change detection on the source data flags when a generated calendar must be re-derived (another Company Object projection).
  • Escalation policy is explicit and tested: who gets told, on which channels, after how long, and what happens on silence.
§05 · Provider abstraction

Swap safely, or it isn't really swappable

The module depends only on the port. A registry reads the tenant's choice and returns an adapter. For the MVP you ship only third-party adapters; the interface is the contract that makes "first-party later" a one-file change. But "swap safely" is only true with three additions: a conformance suite every adapter must pass, an anti-corruption layer so a vendor's schema never leaks into your domain, and capability negotiation because no two providers support an identical feature set.

§06 · Reliability

Long waits, hard failures, money and government

These workflows wait days and weeks, and several nodes are explicitly marked critical. The orchestration core earns its keep here.

  • Durable execution (Temporal / Inngest / DBOS, or DB-backed sagas + a queue) so a wait for the IRS or a founder's signature survives every deploy and crash. This is the single biggest risk if hand-rolled.
  • Transactional outbox for triggers — the cross-workflow gate commits atomically with the state change, so "entity formed" is never lost.
  • Saga compensation for the red paths — a rejected Delaware filing has a defined rollback-and-retry, not an orphaned half-state.
  • Idempotency keys on every external mutation — you never double-file with the IRS or double-charge a card because a webhook retried.
  • Circuit breakers + dead-letter queues per adapter — third-party tools will go down; degrade visibly instead of hanging.
§07 · Trust

A fiduciary product, secured like one

You hold EINs, SSNs, cap tables, and OAuth tokens into founders' bank, Stripe, and GitHub accounts. Security is a product feature here, not a backlog item.

  • Tenant isolation enforced at the database (row-level security), with the option to graduate sensitive tenants to schema- or DB-per-tenant.
  • Field-level encryption for PII and secrets — EIN, SSN, bank details, and provider tokens encrypted distinctly from ordinary data.
  • Scoped, rotated tokens per adapter — least privilege, short-lived, revocable per connection.
  • Tamper-evident audit — the hash-chained log doubles as legal evidence and SOC 2 material.
  • Data residency — EU customers (your W2 already generates a DPA) mean region-aware storage from the start.
  • SOC 2 by design — audit trails, access control, encryption, and change management are cheap now and brutal to retrofit.
§08 · The product line

Variation, made explicit

The PLE engine is a real component, not a vibe. A variation model spans entity type × jurisdiction × industry × provider choices × plan tier. A configurator assembles each tenant's active workflow set, rule-packs, and adapters from their selections. A UK LLC and a Delaware C-corp are two configurations of one platform — not two codebases.

One subtlety that bites later: workflow versioning. Definitions evolve, but a founder halfway through W1 must finish on the version they started; new tenants get the new version. Pin the definition at instance start and migrate deliberately.

§10 · Build order

The MVP cut line

Prove the spine on one workflow before scaling to eight. Third-party adapters only; first-party deferred entirely.

Platform spine. Durable workflow engine + event-sourced Company Object + outbox trigger bus + multi-tenant auth. Nothing founder-facing yet — this is the thing everything else stands on.
Port framework. Define the port interfaces, the registry, and the conformance harness. Build one adapter each for the critical capabilities: incorporation, banking, e-sign, payments.
Document intelligence v1. Grounded generation → deterministic validators → human approval → versioned store, with a starter eval suite. The legal/financial safety net comes online here.
W1 end-to-end. Ship Business Formation as the reference workflow — it gates everything and exercises the entire spine, including the 83(b) fuse.
Compliance engine. Rule-packs + the guaranteed-deadline path, validated against the 83(b) case.
W2–W8 as manifests. With the spine proven, the rest are largely declarative — the 30% — reusing everything above.
Dogfood it. Build StartupKit on the same kind of providers it sells — Temporal, Clerk/WorkOS, Stripe, a managed Postgres. It de-risks the build and is a genuinely good founder story for YC.
